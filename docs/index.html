Startup roadmap

A startup is not just a small company or a clever app. It is a team searching for a repeatable way to solve a meaningful problem—and a sustainable way to deliver that solution.

01 · Problem

Find a real struggle

Start with a specific person, a repeated frustration, and the cost of leaving it unsolved.

02 · Evidence

Replace guesses with learning

Talk to people, study current workarounds, and look for behavior—not polite compliments.

03 · Experiment

Test the smallest promise

Use a prototype, landing page, pilot, or manual service before building the full product.

Problem to solution

Problem statement

Make the problem narrow enough to test

[Specific user] struggles to [make progress] when [situation] because [root cause], which leads to [measurable cost].

“Students need a productivity app” is broad. “First-year commuters miss campus events because information is scattered across five channels” is testable.

Solution hypothesis

Describe an outcome, not a feature pile

We believe [solution] will help [user] achieve [outcome]. We will know when [behavior or metric] changes.

Your first solution is a hypothesis. Learning that it is wrong early is progress, not failure.

Quick problem check

Ideas and brainstorming

Strong startup ideas usually come from close observation: inconvenient workflows, excluded users, expensive delays, new technology, or a process you understand unusually well.

Observe

Keep a friction log

Record repeated complaints, awkward workarounds, waiting, waste, and tasks people avoid.

Reframe

Ask “How might we?”

Turn a complaint into an open challenge without deciding the answer too early.

Expand

Create 20 options

Separate idea generation from judgment. Include manual, service, software, and community solutions.

Narrow

Score the evidence

Rank urgency, access to users, willingness to act, testability, and your unfair insight.

15-minute idea sprint

Choose one community you know. List five repeated problems, three current workarounds for each, and one way to test demand this week for the strongest problem.

Validation and MVP

Customer discovery

Interview the past, not the imaginary future

  • “Tell me about the last time this happened.”
  • “What did you do instead?”
  • “What did that cost in time, money, or stress?”
  • “Who decides whether to try or buy a solution?”

Avoid pitching during discovery. The goal is to understand behavior and decision-making.

Minimum viable product

Build the smallest credible test

  • Clickable prototype for usability
  • Landing page or waitlist for demand
  • Manual concierge service for delivery
  • Paid pilot or preorder for willingness to pay

An MVP is a learning instrument. Choose it based on the riskiest assumption—not the most exciting feature.

Data

Protect customer information

Collect only what you need, secure access, explain usage clearly, and review rules for minors, health, education, payments, and location data.

People

Know who works for you

Employee and contractor classifications have different tax, wage, benefit, and IP consequences.

Financial foundations

You do not need an MBA to understand startup finance. You do need a clear view of cash, costs, price, unit economics, and how long you can keep learning.

Costs

Separate one-time and monthly costs

Include software, equipment, licenses, insurance, contractors, marketing, payment fees, and a realistic buffer.

Price

Connect price to value

Test willingness to pay. Revenue is stronger evidence than sign-ups, and margin matters more than revenue alone.

Cash

Track burn and runway

Net burn is monthly cash out minus cash in. Runway estimates how many months remain at the current burn.

Funding

Match money to the milestone

Bootstrapping, grants, competitions, loans, and equity each change risk, control, and expectations.

What can you responsibly estimate?

With consistent historical data, models can estimate retention risk, demand, revenue ranges, churn, cash runway, or a defined success score. They cannot know whether a startup will “succeed overall” with certainty. Predictions inherit the assumptions, missing information, and bias in their training data.

Research lab

R + LASSO

Test startup signals with data

The learning guides above help students make decisions today. This research lab asks a narrower question: which observed startup characteristics are associated with a defined Success Score?

Two models are prepared. The earlier-stage model uses age, country, industry, funding stage, total funding, and employee count. The all-observed model adds annual revenue and valuation. Because revenue and valuation may be consequences of success, the second model is descriptive rather than causal.

Dataset overview

The dashboard begins with a basic data-quality check before showing any model results. Missing values, sample size, and category coverage matter because a clean-looking graph can still be misleading when the underlying sample is thin.

Factor comparisons

Pearson correlation measures linear association; Spearman correlation also detects monotonic relationships and is less sensitive to extreme values. Correlation does not establish that a factor causes startup success.

Country, industry, and funding-stage relationships

Group averages can be unstable for categories represented by few startups. Accordingly, each plot shows the group sample size and only displays the 12 largest groups.

LASSO regression

The data are split 80/20 into training and test sets. All imputation, dummy encoding, removal of zero-variance columns, and standardization are learned only from the training data. Cross-validation selects the penalty. The conservative lambda.1se value is used for the final model; it usually produces a smaller, more stable set of predictors than the minimum-error penalty.

Out-of-sample performance

A useful model should have RMSE below the mean-only baseline on held-out data. Negative test R-squared means the model generalized worse than simply predicting the test-set mean.

Selected factors

Because inputs are standardized, coefficient magnitudes are broadly comparable. A positive coefficient is associated with a higher predicted success score, holding the other included variables constant; a zero coefficient was excluded by LASSO. Category coefficients are relative to the omitted reference category.

Prediction check

Research conclusions

  • These results identify associations, not causal effects. Funding, hiring, revenue, valuation, and success may influence one another.
  • The startup name is excluded because it is an identifier, not a reusable explanatory factor.
  • LASSO may choose one variable from a group of highly correlated variables and shrink the others to zero. A zero coefficient does not prove that a factor is irrelevant.
  • Country and industry estimates need adequate representation. Results for small categories are unstable and should not drive decisions alone.
  • The held-out test metrics are the best guide to whether the relationships generalize beyond this sample.
  • A stronger causal analysis would require time-stamped predictors measured before the success outcome, a clear definition of Success Score, and preferably repeated observations over time.

Research methodology

The analysis uses an 80/20 training-test split. Preprocessing is learned from the training data only: missing numeric values are median-imputed, new and missing categories are handled explicitly, categorical variables are dummy encoded, and numeric predictors are standardized. Ten-fold cross-validation (or fewer folds for small samples) selects the LASSO penalty. The conservative one-standard-error penalty is reported to favor a simpler model.

The full R source is available in the project repository so every figure and metric can be rebuilt when the dataset changes.

Reproducibility details

The report was generated with R and records its complete package environment in the source build. This keeps the public page focused while preserving the technical details in the repository.
